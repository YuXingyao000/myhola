# A used personal repair workshop, derived from The Shed

Use `reference_mode: adapt`. Create a small, believable repair workshop using the
supplied The Shed project and its assets. The result should approach the reference's material, construction,
and spatial quality. Make a reusable room that can be photographed from several
positions. This stage contains no target CAD part or reserved product display area.

## Activity and adaptation

The owner is maintaining a bench vise and organizing hand tools during an ordinary
afternoon. If the supplied project lacks a convincing vise, choose a comparable
repair activity supported by its mature assets and explain the choice in the plan.
The workshop has been used for years and remains functional. It is neither a new
showroom nor an abandoned, uniformly dirty space.

Make a meaningful variation of the supplied workshop. Reorganize the relationship
between the main working area and nearby tool storage, and arrange a credible
ongoing task with relevant tools, containers, or a cloth. Adapt the practical and
daylight arrangement to that workspace. Retain the room shell and high-quality
assets where useful. Document what was inherited and what changed. Reusing the
source unchanged with new cameras does not fulfill this brief.

## Layout and furnishings

Provide a plausible working position, reachable storage, floor space for access,
and a place to put tools during use. Preserve complete asset groups and credible
dimensions. Concentrate activity around the task and storage areas, while allowing
some quieter space. Avoid even spacing, decorative rings of props, a deliberately
empty central platform, or unrelated objects scattered solely to increase density.

Use the supplied detailed furniture and tools for prominent elements. Keep their
geometry, UV layouts, and material maps. Additional simple construction may fill
real spatial needs, but do not replace recognizable detailed tools with crude
primitive approximations. Walls, flooring, shelves, and the workbench should hold
up in both context views and closer inspection.

## Materials and condition

Preserve the distinct responses of wood, painted metal, bare steel, plastic,
fabric, and masonry where those materials occur. Inspect the source maps before
adding wear. A used bench can show localized scratches and cleaned stains; a tool
handle can have handling wear; a sheltered shelf can have some dust. Keep these
effects tied to use rather than applying a universal grime layer. Maintain correct
texture scale and convincing roughness and normal detail.

## Lighting and photography

Use plausible afternoon daylight through the existing openings, with practical
lighting suitable for close manual work when needed. Arrange illumination around
the actual room and activity. Preserve contact, readable dark regions, convincing
reflections, and differences between material types. Avoid an evenly illuminated
display studio, excessive orange tint, large clipped highlights, or darkness that
conceals unfinished areas. Treat the view through any visible opening coherently.

Create all six inspection views required by the contract. Show the whole room
from meaningfully different directions, the working activity in context, and
close views of important surfaces and objects. Use reasonable camera positions
and fields of view. The environment must remain intact and convincing across
them. Do not use strong depth of field to hide material or geometry deficiencies.

Deliver the rebuildable template and real preview evidence through the supplied
workflow. State any remaining limitation concretely. Describe the result as a
The Shed-derived environment and preserve the provided source attribution.
